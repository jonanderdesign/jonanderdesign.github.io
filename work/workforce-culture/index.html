The problem

The acquired company’s product measures an organisation’s culture with a survey and uses AI to explain the results, and it had to become part of Factorial’s product.

Two conditions shaped the design. The answers are about people, so nobody could be identified, no matter how the results were filtered. And the team set that results had to be explainable (under the EU AI Act): the product calculates the score and the AI only writes the explanation.

The key idea

Hiding small groups is not enough when filters combine: subtracting one result from another can reveal them, a differencing attack. Groups with fewer than three answers were already hidden, but with two filters combined the total said seven answers and only six showed on screen. Subtracting one from the other revealed that the only manager in that group had answered.

A static mock-up would not have shown it; a working prototype did. The AI writing the code advised leaving it. I decided to fix it before anything shipped: the AI proposed a method (from a known survey tool), and it was fixed in the three places the leak appeared.

The fewer-than-three rule now also covers subtraction: with any filter, a small group can be neither read nor worked out. The cost is that small groups show less data.

What changed

At first I made it a permission closed by default: only the person who creates the survey sees the culture results. That fails companies that want their staff to see them: a company asks its people about its culture, and they never see the answer.

No fixed setting fits every company, so who sees the results became a permission each company sets. Engineering made admins the default.

Result

Launch planned for November 2026.

Before launch, the prototype was the team’s reference for the integration, an engineer built the survey from it, and Sales was showing it to customers.

The brief was in Claude Code: the product documentation and the tickets, read through MCP, and the two conditions the team had set, anonymity and explainability. I compiled the production design system from source into Claude Design and shared the set-up with the design team; the culture assessment took 177 artboards (159 of them built from production components), and the results page went through three rounds and seven versions before choosing.

The prototype was real code, so it found the leak; a mock-up would have shown nothing. The AI writing the code advised leaving it and adding it to a question for Legal. I had it fixed in the three places it appeared, before anything shipped.

In October, several agents audited the prototype: 102 findings, 100 of them confirmed by a second reviewer. The prototype became the team’s reference, an engineer built the survey from it, and Sales was showing it to customers before launch.
