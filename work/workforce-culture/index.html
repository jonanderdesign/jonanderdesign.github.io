Workforce Culture.

Factorial bought a culture assessment that uses AI to explain its results. This is how it became a product inside Factorial, with anonymity that holds no matter how the results are filtered.

Overview

Company
Factorial, HR software
Team
A product manager, two engineers and the product design director
When
September to November 2026
Result
A culture assessment inside Factorial with results that stay anonymous no matter how you filter them, launching in November 2026

Screens are from the prototype and use invented data.

The problem

The acquired product measures an organisation’s culture through a survey and uses AI to explain the results.

Factorial sells payroll and HR administration; assessing people was not part of it. The acquisition opened that line of work, and brought with it large German customers already running the assessment. For the purchase to pay off, it had to run on the people a company already manages in Factorial. Two conditions defined the design from the start.

Anonymity under any filter. The answers are about people, so no one may be identifiable, no matter how the results are filtered.

Explainable results. Under the EU AI Act the results have to be explainable, and that part was settled before the design began: the product calculates the score and the AI only writes the explanation. Anonymity was the condition that remained open.

The decisions

Decision 1: Anonymity as a subtraction rule.

Groups with fewer than three answers were already hidden. However, when two filters were combined, the total showed seven answers and the screen only six: by subtracting one from the other, anyone could tell that the only manager in that group had answered. This is known as a differencing attack, and it only shows up when the totals are actually computed, which is why the working prototype caught it and a static mock-up would not have.

I extended the rule to cover subtraction, so that with any filter active a group with fewer than three answers can neither be read nor deduced, and the same rule applies in the three places where the leak appeared. The trade-off is that small groups show less data.

What changed

Decision 2: Who sees the results is up to each company.

The first version restricted the results to the person who created the survey. That does not hold for companies that want their employees to see them: a company asks its people about its culture and then never shows them the answer.

Since no fixed setting fits every company, I made access a permission that each company configures, with administrators as the default.

Result

Launching in November 2026.

The assessment runs inside Factorial on the people a company already manages there, with results that stay anonymous no matter how they are filtered and explanations that rest on a score the product itself calculates. Sales was showing it to customers before launch.

The design work was managed from Claude Code, connected to the product documentation and the tickets through MCP, with the two conditions as the brief. I compiled the production design system from source into Claude Design and shared the setup with the design team, so the prototype was built from the same components as the product itself. Because of that, the engineer built the survey from it rather than from a specification.

The easy route with the leak was to log it as a question for Legal and move on. I did not take it. The promise to the employee is that no one can be identified, and a leak that two filters can demonstrate breaks that promise whatever Legal says, so it was fixed before anything shipped.
